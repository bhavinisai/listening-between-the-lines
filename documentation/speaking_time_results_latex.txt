\section*{Abstract}

    Podcasts are a rapidly growing form of digital media, especially in India, where they provide rich, multi-speaker conversational content. In this work, we provide an automated pipeline that combines gender detection, host-guest role identification, transcription, and speaker diarization to examine conversational flow in Indian podcasts. Using this framework, we analyze speaking time distribution and interaction patterns across episodes. Our results highlight gender-based disparities in interaction patterns and demonstrate that conversations are primarily guest-driven, with guests speaking much more than hosts. This work provides a structured approach for understanding real-world conversational dynamics in podcasts.

\section*{Achievements from DSCI-602 }

In the last semester, we worked on building a dataset of Indian podcast transcripts with accurate labels for the gender and role of each speaker. We first created a pipeline to scrape podcast audio files from YouTube, after which we used deep learning tools such as WhisperX and Pyannote for speaker diarization. This led to the generation of transcripts with basic speaker segmentation and corresponding timestamps. We then used a script to identify and label the gender of each speaker based on the frequency characteristics of their voice.

The next step involved identifying the host and guest in each transcript, which we implemented using the Grok API. The resulting corpus enabled us to begin identifying conversational structures, such as turn-taking and host-versus-guest dominance, and to quantify conversational dynamics, although with some limitations in accuracy. Overall, the work completed during the semester resulted in a basic but functional prototype pipeline for podcast analysis. Using this pipeline, we built a corpus of approximately $\sim 500$  transcripts.

\section*{Dataset}
The dataset consists of \textbf{418} English- and Hindi-language Indian
podcast episodes collected from YouTube, about \textbf{534 hours} of audio in
total (median episode length 70 minutes, interquartile range 54--93 minutes).
Every episode is a one-on-one conversation between a host and a guest.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Transcripts:} each episode was transcribed and diarized with
    WhisperX, giving timestamped segments attributed to speakers (about 5.1
    million words in total; median 11{,}300 words per episode).
  \item \textbf{Host and guest roles:} the host was identified by matching
    speakers' voices to a library of 9 recurring hosts (4 female, 5 male),
    which covers 389 episodes; in the remaining 29 episodes the host was
    identified with a language-model fallback.
  \item \textbf{Speaker gender:} taken from the voice library for known hosts,
    and otherwise predicted from the speaker's voice; two guests whose gender
    could not be determined were labeled manually.
  \item \textbf{Exclusions:} one episode that duplicated another was removed.
\end{itemize}
Episodes are grouped by the gender of the host and of the guest
(Table~\ref{tab:dataset-dyads}); male-hosted conversations with male guests
make up more than half of the corpus.

\subsection*{Host and guest speaking time by gender dyad}
\paragraph{Question:}
How is speaking time divided between the host and the guest, and does the
division depend on the genders of the host and the guest?

\paragraph{Experiment:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
  \item For each of the 418 episodes, a speaker's speaking time is the sum of
    the durations (end minus start) of their diarized WhisperX segments.
  \item The host's share of speaking time in an episode is
    \[
      \text{Host share} = \frac{T_{\text{host}}}{T_{\text{host}} + T_{\text{guest}}},
    \]
    where $T_{\text{host}}$ and $T_{\text{guest}}$ are the host's and the
    guest's speaking times. Silence and non-speech are excluded.
  \item Episodes are grouped by host$\rightarrow$guest gender dyad. Because
    the shares are skewed, each dyad is summarized by its median and
    interquartile range (IQR).
  \item As a robustness check, the host's share is also computed from word
    counts instead of time.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Findings:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Guests do most of the talking:} The median host takes only
    19--26\% of the host-plus-guest speaking time in every dyad
    (Table~\ref{tab:speaking-time}, Figures~\ref{fig:speaking-time-minutes}
    and~\ref{fig:speaking-time-share-distribution}).
  \item \textbf{Male hosts talk more with female guests:} their median share is
    26.3\% with female guests against 21.3\% with male guests.
  \item \textbf{Female hosts do not change with guest gender} (19.8\% vs.\
    19.0\%). This is a null result.
  \item \textbf{Male-hosted episodes are longer:} about 64--69 minutes of host
    and guest speech, against 43--46 minutes with female hosts.

\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Statistical comparison of host speaking time across dyads}
\paragraph{Question:}
Are the differences in host share between the dyads (previous section)
larger than would be expected by chance? Rank-based tests are used because
the shares are skewed and the dyads are unequal in size (61--226 episodes);
the Holm correction keeps the chance of a false positive across the six
pairwise tests at 5\%, and the rank-biserial correlation $r$ ($-1$ to $+1$,
$0$ = no difference) shows how large each difference is.

\paragraph{Experiments:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Confidence intervals:} a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval
    of each dyad's median host share (10{,}000 resamples of episodes).
  \item \textbf{Overall test:} a Kruskal--Wallis test of whether the host
    share differs across the four dyads.
  \item \textbf{Pairwise tests:} two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ tests for the six
    pairs of dyads, Holm-corrected, with the rank-biserial correlation $r$ as
    the effect size.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Findings:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The dyads differ:} The Kruskal--Wallis test shows that the
    host's share of speaking time differs across the four dyads, and the
    pairwise Mann--Whitney $U$ tests, after Holm correction, show which pairs
    differ (Table~\ref{tab:speaking-time-ci},

  \item \textbf{No difference between Female$\rightarrow$female and Female$\rightarrow$male:} After Holm correction, the pairwise tests
    find no difference between female$\rightarrow$male and
    female$\rightarrow$female episodes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Conversational style by gender dyad}
\paragraph{Question:}
Do hosts and guests speak differently depending on the host$\rightarrow$guest
gender dyad: how often they hedge, emphasize, present statements as fact, ask
direct questions and defer to the other speaker? The dyads are compared with
the same rank-based tests as speaking time. Comparing the two dyads with a
host of the same gender (male$\rightarrow$male vs.\ male$\rightarrow$female,
and female$\rightarrow$male vs.\ female$\rightarrow$female) shows whether the
guest's gender matters.
\vspace{2mm}
\paragraph{Experiment:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Features:} \emph{Hedges} (e.g.\ \emph{I think}, \emph{maybe},
    \emph{probably}) and \emph{boosters} (e.g.\ \emph{really}, \emph{of
    course}, \emph{definitely}) are taken from Hyland's metadiscourse
    lexicon~\cite{hyland2005metadiscourse}. \emph{Factuality} (e.g.\
    \emph{actually}, \emph{in fact}), \emph{deference} (e.g.\
    \emph{interesting}, \emph{nice}, \emph{great question}) and \emph{direct
    questions} (wh-questions) are politeness
    strategies~\cite{danescu2013politeness} as implemented in
    ConvoKit~\cite{chang2020convokit}.
  \item \textbf{Rates:} For the host and the guest of each of the 418
    episodes, each feature is counted and expressed as a rate per 1{,}000
    words.
  \item \textbf{Accuracy audit:} For each feature, 50--100 randomly sampled
    occurrences were checked by hand: 71--86\% were correct uses (hedges
    86\%, boosters 71\%, factuality 76\%, deference 82\%, host direct
    questions 97\%). Guests' direct questions were mostly rhetorical, so
    direct questions are analyzed for hosts only.
  \item \textbf{Dyad tests:} For each of the 9 feature-by-speaker measures, a
    Kruskal--Wallis test across the four dyads (Holm-corrected across the 9
    tests) and pairwise Mann--Whitney $U$ tests (Holm-corrected across the six
    pairs). Two of the six pairs keep the host's gender fixed and change only
    the guest's (male$\rightarrow$male vs.\ male$\rightarrow$female, and
    female$\rightarrow$male vs.\ female$\rightarrow$female); these test the
    effect of guest gender.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Findings:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Most measures differ across dyads:} The Kruskal--Wallis tests
    (Holm-corrected) show that almost every feature differs across the four
    dyads, and the pairwise Mann--Whitney $U$ tests, after Holm correction,
    show which pairs of dyads differ (Table~\ref{tab:style-tests}).
  \item \textbf{Hedging (host): no difference (negative finding):} The
    Kruskal--Wallis test on the host's hedging rate per 1{,}000 words is not
    significant: hosts hedge at a similar rate in all dyads.
  \item \textbf{Hedging (guest): depends on the host's gender, not the
    guest's:} The median hedging rates and the pairwise tests show that guests
    hedge more with a female host than with a male host
    (Figure~\ref{fig:style-rates-iqr}). The comparisons that keep the host's
    gender fixed find no difference between female and male guests.
  \item \textbf{Boosters:} The median booster rates and the pairwise tests show
    that boosters are more frequent in episodes with a female host, for both
    hosts and guests, with female$\rightarrow$female episodes the most
    emphatic. With hosts of either gender, female guests use more boosters
    than male guests; hosts' own use of boosters does not differ by the
    guest's gender.
  \item \textbf{Factuality:} The median factuality rates and the pairwise tests
    show that guests in female$\rightarrow$female episodes use factuality
    markers most, more than guests in either male-host dyad. Hosts use them
    more in the female-host dyads than in male$\rightarrow$male episodes. For
    hosts and guests alike, the comparisons that keep the host's gender fixed
    find no difference by guest gender.
  \item \textbf{Direct questions:} The median rates of host direct questions
    and the pairwise tests show that hosts ask more direct questions of guests
    of the other gender: male hosts ask female guests more than male guests,
    and female hosts ask male guests more than female guests
    (Figure~\ref{fig:style-questions-deference}). Both comparisons are
    significant.
  \item \textbf{Deference:} The share of episodes in which deference is used,
    together with the pairwise tests on the deference rate, shows that
    acknowledgements such as \emph{interesting} or \emph{nice} are far more
    common in episodes with a male host, for both hosts and guests. The
    comparisons that keep the host's gender fixed find no difference by guest
    gender, for hosts or guests.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Dialogue acts by gender dyad}
\paragraph{Question:}
Does what hosts and guests \emph{do} in their turns (ask, answer, make
statements, acknowledge) differ by host$\rightarrow$guest gender dyad? The
dyads are compared with the same rank-based tests as before.

\paragraph{Experiment:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Labeling:} Every transcript segment of the host and the guest
    was labeled with one of 11 dialogue acts by a pretrained classifier
    (\texttt{diwank/silicone-deberta-pair}, a DeBERTa model fine-tuned on the
    SILICONE dialogue-act benchmark), which reads each segment together with
    the preceding segment as context. Unlike the style features, the unit is
    the individual transcript segment: segments are not merged into turns,
    and the first and last minute of each episode are included.
  \item \textbf{Shares:} For each episode we compute, separately for the host
    and the guest, the share of their segments that carry each act. The
    comparisons use the six acts that make up at least 1\% of all segments
    (Table~\ref{tab:da-dist}); the other five are too rare to compare per
    episode.
  \item \textbf{Dyad tests:} Medians with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals,
    a Kruskal--Wallis test per act and speaker (Holm-corrected across the
    twelve tests), and pairwise Mann--Whitney $U$ tests (Holm-corrected across
    the six pairs) with the rank-biserial correlation $r$ as the effect size.
\end{enumerate}

    shows that hosts ask far more questions than guests, while guests' segments
    are mostly answers and statements (Table~\ref{tab:da-dist},
    Figure~\ref{fig:da-composition}).

    (Holm-corrected) show that most act-by-speaker measures differ across the
    four dyads (Table~\ref{tab:da}), and the pairwise Mann--Whitney $U$ tests,
    after Holm correction, show which pairs of dyads differ
    (Table~\ref{tab:da-pairs}).
  \item \textbf{Guests: the host's gender matters most:} The median shares of
    each act and the pairwise tests show that, with a female host, guests make
    more statements, give fewer answers, ask fewer questions and express fewer
    intentions than with a male host (Figure~\ref{fig:da-main}).

    their rank-biserial correlations show that, with female hosts, female

    their other acts do not differ.

    female hosts of male guests ask the most questions. Hosts answer less and
    express fewer intentions in episodes with a female host, and make more

  \item \textbf{Negative finding:} The Kruskal--Wallis tests on how often hosts
    acknowledge the guest or reply ``yes'' are not significant: these acts do
    not differ between dyads.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Topic Control and Follow-up in Mixed-Gender Dyads}

Topic control is an important aspect of who dominates a conversation. The speaker whose topics the other person picks up usually steers the direction of the conversation. A long-standing idea in language and gender research is that men's topics are more likely to be taken up, whereas women's topics are more likely to be ignored or dropped.

Recent studies suggest that gender still shapes topic initiation \cite{TopicInitiationbyKoreanandChineseSpeakersonRealityDatingShows}, but large-scale studies indicate that gendered speech patterns depend on context and are not always male-dominated \cite{yoo2022conversational}. We test whether this pattern also appears in Indian podcasts (English and Hindi) featuring one host and one guest. Podcasts are well suited to this question because they include long and natural conversations between speakers. In addition, hosts also steer the discussion by asking questions and guests answer them. This helps us examine whether follow-up is associated with the speaker's gender or with their conversational role.

Our analysis focuses on mixed-gender host-guest conversations and compares how often male-initiated topics receive follow-up with how often female-initiated topics do. Since podcast transcripts do not label where the topic changes, topic initiation and follow-up are identified automatically using lexical similarity between conversational turns, with semantic similarity used as a robustness check.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ:} In mixed-gender host--guest conversations, are topics introduced by male speakers followed up more often than topics introduced by female speakers?
\vspace{-0.8em}
\end{quote}

Based on prior research, we develop the following hypotheses:
\vspace{-0.8em}
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H$_1$:} Male-initiated topics are followed up more than female-initiated topics.

\textbf{H$_0$:} Male-initiated topics are not followed up more than female-initiated topics.
\end{quote}

The hypothesis test uses the \textbf{131 mixed-gender episodes} (67 MF + 64 FM; 262 speakers), which contain \textbf{17,046 topic initiations} with a response: \textbf{8,742 by male speakers} and \textbf{8,304 by female speakers}. Each of these episodes has one male and one female speaker, and also one host and one guest. This means that within an episode, gender and role are perfectly linked. So the only way to compare men and women in the same role is to compare different episodes (male-hosted vs.\ female-hosted).

\subsubsection*{Identifying initiations and follow-ups}
Consecutive lines from the same speaker were merged into one turn, and backchannels such as ``yeah'' and ``mhm'' were removed. Each turn was represented as a TF-IDF vector, and cosine similarity measured the lexical overlap between adjacent turns.

A turn was treated as a topic initiation if its similarity to the previous turn was at or below the episode median, and as followed up if the other speaker's next turn was more similar to it than the median. Medians were computed separately for each episode to allow for differences between conversations.

However, most consecutive turns share no content words, so the median is often zero. In 72 of the 131 episodes, a follow-up therefore just means that the reply repeats at least one content word from the initiating turn. Therefore, the measure captures \textbf{lexical overlap} rather than topic continuation as a listener would judge it, and it has not been validated against human judgements.


\subsubsection*{Statistical Analysis}
Because turns within an episode are not independent, the primary analysis is a \textbf{GEE logistic regression} that predicts follow-up from the initiator's gender, controlling for role (host or guest) and turn length, with errors clustered by episode. Since gender and role are linked within an episode, the gender effect in this model comes only from comparing male-hosted and female-hosted episodes. A \textbf{sensitivity analysis} therefore varied one choice at a time: how turn length is included, how episodes are weighted, and whether errors are clustered by host instead of episode. The host--guest gap was also compared directly between male-hosted and female-hosted episodes.

Paired tests compared the male and female speakers within each episode, and a mixed-effects model was also fitted. These answer related but different questions: the paired tests do not adjust for role, and the mixed model uses the continuous similarity score instead of the binary follow-up. The paired test was repeated with sentence embeddings, which capture meaning rather than word overlap.


\subsubsection*{Findings}

\paragraph{Raw comparison:}
After pooling across all initiations, male-initiated topics appear to be followed
up more often (42.2\% vs.\ 37.7\%, a gap of \textbf{+4.4 pp}; Table~\ref{tab:tc-descriptive}). This pooled gap appears at every fixed similarity threshold, but it does not take role into account.
However, within the same episode there is no clear gap (41.5\% vs.\ 42.6\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:tc-episode}). Fig.~\ref{fig:tc-episode} shows why: the male speaker is followed up more when he is the host (+9.4 pp) and less when he is the guest ($-12.3$ pp), and these cancel out.

\paragraph{Hypothesis test:}
The GEE results do not support H$_1$ (Table~\ref{tab:tc-models}). After controlling for role and turn length, host-initiated topics have higher odds of follow-up than guest-initiated topics (OR $= 2.20$). In the original model, male-initiated topics have slightly lower odds of follow-up (OR $= 0.85$, $p = 0.002$), which is the opposite of what H$_1$ predicts.

However, the sensitivity analysis shows that this gender result is not robust (Table~\ref{tab:tc-sensitivity}). The odds ratio for men ranges from \textbf{0.79 to 1.00} depending on how turn length is included, and it is not significant when turn length is left out ($p = 0.94$), when reply length is added ($p = 0.13$), or when errors are grouped by host ($p = 0.08$). The direct episode-level comparison also finds no clear difference ($p = 0.22$). In contrast, the host association stays above 1 in every version (OR 1.50--2.78). The within-episode tests, with either TF-IDF or embeddings ($p = 0.44$ and $p = 0.43$), and the mixed-effects model ($p = 0.42$) also find no clear gender difference, although they answer slightly different questions. \textbf{We therefore do not reject H$_0$}, and we do not treat the OR of 0.85 as evidence that men's topics are followed up less. A non-significant result here also does not show that men and women are followed up equally.

\paragraph{Role across all dyads:}
Across all episodes (42,320 initiations), the host-initiated topics are
followed up more than guest-initiated topics in every dyad (46--52\% vs.\
36--41\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:tc-dyad}), whether the host is male or female. Part of this association comes from how the measure works: questions get more lexical follow-up than other turns, hosts ask more questions, and longer replies are more likely to share a word with the turn before them. So when a guest gives a long answer to a host's question, it usually counts as a follow-up.

\subsubsection*{Conclusion}

We found no evidence that men's topics are followed up more than women's. The male advantage in the pooled data appears because most topics started by men come from hosts (78\%), whereas most topics started by women come from guests (only 30\% from hosts). Role, rather than gender, is therefore more closely linked to whose topics are taken up. This does not rule out a gender effect, however, as the gender estimate is sensitive to model specification and rests on a small number of hosts. Our measure also relies on word overlap between adjacent turns and has not been validated by human annotators, so it may miss replies that continue a topic in different words. Overall, the findings support the view that gendered speech patterns are context-dependent \cite{yoo2022conversational}, and show that ignoring speaker role and episode structure could have suggested a gender bias that the data do not support.

\subsection*{Question Types and Guest Gender}

Questions are one of the main ways a host shapes the interview. The type of question asked (e.g., open or closed, personal or professional) decides what a guest talks about and how much room they get to answer.
Media research has shown that women are often depicted by their personal life and looks rather than their skills and abilities \cite{Andrich-2023}\cite{bauer_taylor_2023}. We look at whether hosts of English-language Indian podcasts ask different kinds of questions based on the guest's gender.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ:} Does the type of question asked by the host
differ by the gender of the guest?
\vspace{-0.8em}
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H$_1$:} The distribution of question type differs by guest's gender.

\textbf{H$_0$:} The distribution of question type does not differ by guest's
gender.
\end{quote}

\subsubsection*{Method}

\paragraph{Data:}
This analysis currently uses the balanced sample of 200 episodes (50 per
dyad: MM, MF, FM, FF). We extracted 27,299 questions, of which 12,726 were
asked by hosts. Only host questions are analysed.

\paragraph{Question extraction:}
We combined any lines spoken by the same speaker into one turn. Each of the turns was then split into sentences, and sentences ending with a question mark are kept as questions.

\paragraph{Question types:}
Each question is assigned one of six types (Table~\ref{tab:qt-types}).

\subsection*{Sentiment and Emotional Dynamics in Gender Dyads}

A second way speakers shape a conversation is through emotional tone. If one speaker becomes more positive, negative or intense, the other speaker might match that tone, push back against it, or try to change it. If listeners match the tone of men more than that of women, men would have a greater influence on the emotional course of a conversation. We examine this in Indian podcasts through three behaviours: \textbf{mirroring} (how closely a speaker's tone follows the previous turn), \textbf{escalation} (whether positivity decreases or intensity increases across turns), and \textbf{recovery} (which speaker brings the tone back up after a negative turn).


\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ:} Does emotional tone vary across gender dyads, and do guests mirror the tone of male hosts more than that of female hosts?
\vspace{-0.8em}
\end{quote}

Based on prior research, we develop the following hypotheses:
\vspace{-0.8em}
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H$_1$:} Guests mirror the tone of male hosts more than that of female hosts.

\textbf{H$_0$:} Guests do not mirror the tone of male hosts more than that of female hosts.
\end{quote}


Because tone by dyad, escalation, and recovery are exploratory, we made no hypotheses for them. The analysis covers 414 episodes (69,486 turns) across the four host$\rightarrow$guest dyads. We prepared turns as in the topic-control analysis: we merged consecutive lines from the same speaker and removed backchannels.


\subsubsection*{Building the sentiment scorer}
Each turn needed two scores: \textbf{valence} (negative, neutral or positive) and \textbf{arousal} (low, medium or high). Since most of this experiment went into building a scorer we could trust, we describe each step below.

\paragraph{Human gold set:}
We hand-labelled 400 turns from across the corpus, following our predefined guidelines for valence and arousal, and used these labels to evaluate every model. The set turned out to be very unbalanced: 72\% of turns were neutral, and 391 of the 400 turns were medium arousal. A model that always predicts ``neutral'' would already reach 72\% accuracy, so we report macro-F1 and Cohen's $\kappa$ alongside accuracy.

\paragraph{Zero-shot models:}
We compared several pretrained multilingual sentiment models on the gold set without any training. The best one, a multilingual XLM-R model, reached an accuracy of 0.56, a macro-F1 of 0.44 and $\kappa = 0.17$, below the always-neutral baseline. Fine-tuning on the 400 gold labels (with episode-grouped cross-validation) raised accuracy to 0.66--0.69 but did not improve macro-F1 (0.44), so 400 labels were not enough to train a better model.

\paragraph{LLM-labelled training data:}
Since more hand labelling was not possible, we used a large language model (LLM; Claude Sonnet) to label training turns, using a fixed prompt based on the same guidelines as the hand labels. Each turn was labelled in three independent passes, and the majority label was kept. The passes agreed strongly with each other (valence $\kappa = 0.87$, arousal $\kappa = 0.71$ between two passes), but only weakly with our hand labels ($\kappa \approx 0.2$). A first model trained on these labels reached a macro-F1 of 0.71 on held-out LLM-labelled episodes, but only 0.45 on the human gold set (Fig.~\ref{fig:sent-gold}). So the trained models reproduce the LLM's judgement of tone, which is not necessarily the same as a human judgement.

\paragraph{Improving the models:}
Next, we ran three checks to improve the models, using a validation set of 256 turns from 18 episodes that were not part of the training data.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{More data:} we trained on nested sets of 1,783, 3,000 and 5,000 LLM-labelled turns (three seeds each). Arousal improved with more data (macro-F1 0.52 $\rightarrow$ 0.57 $\rightarrow$ 0.62), but valence got slightly worse (0.79 $\rightarrow$ 0.77 $\rightarrow$ 0.75; Fig.~\ref{fig:sent-scaling}).

\item \textbf{Type of added data:} to understand the drop in valence, we added the same number of either targeted turns (likely negative or high-arousal) or random turns to the original 1,783 set (five seeds each). Both additions lowered macro-F1 (0.79 $\rightarrow$ 0.77 and 0.76). Targeted turns improved recall of negative turns compared with random turns, but not compared with the original set.

\item \textbf{Ensembles:} averaging the models from different seeds did not beat the best single model (valence 0.78 vs.\ 0.79; arousal 0.62 vs.\ 0.63).
\end{itemize}

We also tuned decision thresholds on the validation set. This raised the validation scores, but it did not improve results on the test or gold sets, so we did not use it.


\paragraph{Final models:}
We kept the valence model trained on 1,783 turns and the arousal model trained on 5,000 turns (Table~\ref{tab:sent-models}). Their scores are optimistic, because the best epoch of each run was selected using the same 18 validation episodes. These final models were also not evaluated on the human gold set.

\subsubsection*{Measuring emotional dynamics}
Each turn's valence was scored from $-1$ to $1$ (the probability of positive minus the probability of negative), and its arousal from 0 to 1. Using these scores, we measured three behaviours. For \textbf{mirroring}, we measured how closely a reply's valence or arousal followed the previous turn, separately for guests replying to hosts and hosts replying to guests. For \textbf{escalation}, we counted steps where arousal rose or valence fell by at least half a standard deviation from one turn to the next, and runs of at least three turns that kept rising (a total rise of at least one standard deviation). For \textbf{recovery}, we took each negative turn (valence $\le -0.25$) and checked whether the next turn raised valence by at least 0.25, and whether the host or the guest did so.

Since turns within the same episode are not independent, the main analysis is a \textbf{GEE regression} with errors clustered by episode, testing whether a guest's reply follows the host's tone more closely when the host is male. Differences across the four dyads were tested on episode-level values with Kruskal--Wallis tests, and host--guest differences within episodes with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. The main tests were Holm-corrected. Because the scorer has not been validated against human labels, all analyses were repeated with the zero-shot model as a robustness check. As in topic control, gender and role are linked, so comparing male and female hosts also means comparing different shows.


\subsubsection*{Findings}

\paragraph{Tone by dyad:}
Average valence and arousal differ across the four dyads for both hosts and guests (Kruskal--Wallis, all $p < 0.001$; Fig.~\ref{fig:sent-dyad}), and this holds with both scorers.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/fig3a_valence_by_dyad_role.png}
\caption{Average valence of host and guest turns in each dyad (95\% CI).}
\label{fig:sent-dyad}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Mirroring:}
Guests' valence follows the host's valence in every dyad, but more strongly with \textbf{female hosts} than with male hosts, which is the opposite of H$_1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sent-mirror}). With the LLM-trained scorer, this difference is significant after Holm correction ($p = 0.0007$). % CHECK: final-scorer p-value
With the zero-shot scorer, it points in the same direction but is not significant (GEE $p = 0.068$, Holm $p = 0.48$). Arousal mirroring does not differ by host gender ($p = 0.94$). \textbf{We therefore do not reject H$_0$}: there is no evidence that guests mirror male hosts more.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.65\linewidth]{figures/fig5_mirroring_episode_rho.png}
\caption{Per-episode valence mirroring of the guest, by host gender.}
\label{fig:sent-mirror}
\end{figure}



\paragraph{Escalation:}
Hosts are slightly more likely than guests to raise the arousal of the conversation ($\text{OR} = 1.14$, $p < 0.001$), and the rate of escalation steps differs across dyads (Holm $p = 0.025$; Fig.~\ref{fig:sent-escalation}). % CHECK: final-scorer values
In mixed-gender episodes, neither speaker's gender predicts escalation ($p = 0.10$ and $p = 0.55$).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/fig6b_escalation_runs_by_dyad_v2.png}
\caption{Escalation runs per 100 turns in each dyad.}
\label{fig:sent-escalation}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Recovery:}
Recovery results are not consistent between the two scorers (Table~\ref{tab:sent-results}). With one, hosts are less likely than guests to lift the tone after a negative turn ($\text{OR} = 0.76$); with the other, they are more likely ($\text{OR} = 1.18$). We therefore draw no conclusions about who recovers the tone.


\subsubsection*{Conclusion}
We found no evidence that guests mirror the emotional tone of male hosts more than that of female hosts. If anything, guests followed female hosts' tone more closely, although this was only significant with one scorer. Emotional tone does differ across dyads, and hosts are slightly more likely than guests to escalate the conversation. These findings have clear limitations: the scorer was trained on LLM labels that agree only weakly with human labels, arousal could not be validated, and male and female hosts come from different shows. The main methodological finding of this experiment is that, for conversational podcast speech, adding more or more targeted LLM labels did not improve valence scoring.

\subsection*{Interruptions and Overlapping Speech in Gender Dyads}

Interruptions are a third way speakers shape a conversation: by talking over
someone, a speaker can take the floor from them. Not every overlap is an
interruption, however. Short overlaps such as ``yeah'' or ``mhm'' are usually
backchannels that support the current speaker rather than cut them off. We
therefore separate overlaps into backchannels, attempts to take the floor that
fail, and attempts that succeed, and ask whether these differ by
host$\rightarrow$guest gender dyad.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ:} Do interruptions and backchannels differ across gender dyads, and
do male hosts interrupt female guests more than they interrupt male guests?
\vspace{-0.8em}
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H$_1$:} Male hosts interrupt female guests at a different rate than
they interrupt male guests.

\textbf{H$_0$:} Male hosts interrupt female and male guests at the same rate.
\end{quote}

Differences between the other dyads, and between hosts and guests within an
episode, are exploratory, so we made no hypotheses for them.

\subsubsection*{Method}

\paragraph{Data:}
This analysis currently covers episodes 331--418 (88 episodes, about 102
hours of audio). One episode was excluded because its speakers could not be
reliably matched to host and guest (see below), leaving 87 episodes: 37
male$\rightarrow$male, 9 male$\rightarrow$female, 15 female$\rightarrow$male
and 26 female$\rightarrow$female. These episodes come from only four hosts,
two male and two female.

\paragraph{Detecting overlapping speech:}
WhisperX keeps only one speaker at a time, so our transcripts contain no
overlapping speech. We therefore re-ran the same pyannote diarization model
that WhisperX uses (\texttt{speaker-diarization-community-1}, two speakers)
on the audio and kept its overlapping output. Each pyannote speaker was then
matched to the transcript speaker whose words overlap it most in time, which
gives it the host or guest role and gender. Episodes in which less than 70\%
of a speaker's time fell on its matched speaker were excluded (one episode).
In the remaining episodes the median match was 89\% for hosts and 97\% for
guests. Overlapping speech makes up about 5\% of the audio.

\paragraph{Classifying overlaps:}
A speaker's turns less than 0.5\,s apart were merged, so that short pauses
do not count as giving up the floor. An overlap event occurs when speaker B
starts a turn while speaker A is still talking; overlaps shorter than 0.3\,s
were ignored. Each event was assigned one of five types:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Backchannel:} B's turn ends while A is still talking and is
    at most 1\,s long, or contains only backchannel words (e.g.\
    \emph{yeah}, \emph{right}, \emph{haan}).
  \item \textbf{Unsuccessful interruption:} B's turn ends while A is still
    talking, so A keeps the floor.
  \item \textbf{Successful interruption:} B keeps talking after A stops,
    after more than 0.5\,s of overlap, so B takes the floor.
  \item \textbf{Terminal overlap:} B keeps talking after A stops, but the
    overlap is at most 0.5\,s (B starts as A is finishing).
  \item \textbf{Simultaneous start:} both turns start within 0.2\,s of each
    other, so neither speaker clearly overlaps the other.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Rates:}
For each episode, the events started by the host and by the guest were
counted and expressed as a rate per 10 minutes of the \emph{other} speaker's
speaking time, since a speaker can only be interrupted while they hold the
floor. This matters because guests speak about three times as long as hosts
(median 45 vs.\ 15 minutes per episode).

\paragraph{Statistical tests:}
For each of the six measures (successful interruptions, unsuccessful
interruptions and backchannels, each made by the host and by the guest), a
Kruskal--Wallis test compared the four dyads, and pairwise Mann--Whitney $U$
tests compared the six pairs of dyads. The hypothesis is tested by the
male$\rightarrow$male vs.\ male$\rightarrow$female comparison of host
successful interruptions. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test compared the host's and
the guest's rate within each episode. $p$-values were corrected with the
Benjamini--Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure: across the six
measures for the Kruskal--Wallis tests, across the six pairs for each
measure, and across all host-vs-guest tests.

\paragraph{Accuracy check:}
A sample of 200 overlap events has been drawn for checking by ear, but has
not yet been labelled, so the accuracy of the overlap types is not yet known.

\subsubsection*{Findings}

\paragraph{Overall composition:}
The 87 episodes contain 22{,}192 overlap events. Most are backchannels
(56\%), followed by successful interruptions (19\%), unsuccessful
interruptions (15\%), terminal overlaps (6\%) and simultaneous starts (4\%).
Hosts start more overlaps than guests (14{,}081 vs.\ 8{,}111), mainly because
they give far more backchannels (8{,}901 vs.\ 3{,}595). When guests overlap,
they more often take the floor: 29\% of guest overlaps are successful
interruptions, compared with 13\% of host overlaps
(Figure~\ref{fig:int-types}).

\paragraph{Differences across dyads:}
The Kruskal--Wallis tests show that four of the six measures differ across
dyads: successful interruptions by hosts and by guests, unsuccessful
interruptions by hosts, and backchannels by guests
(Table~\ref{tab:int-rates}). Seven of the eight significant pairwise
differences involve the male$\rightarrow$female dyad, which has the highest
median on all six measures, for hosts and guests alike
(Figure~\ref{fig:int-rates}). The two female-host
dyads do not differ from each other on any measure.

\paragraph{Hypothesis test:}
Male hosts make more successful interruptions with female guests than with
male guests (median 7.6 vs.\ 4.4 per 10 minutes; $p_{\text{FDR}} = .030$,
rank-biserial $r = 0.53$), so we reject H$_0$. However, the female guests in
these episodes also interrupt and backchannel more than male guests do. They
give backchannels at almost twice the rate (26.6 vs.\ 14.2;
$p_{\text{FDR}} = .007$), and their successful interruptions (20.7 vs.\ 13.3)
and unsuccessful interruptions (7.7 vs.\ 4.4) are also higher, although not
significantly. Male$\rightarrow$female episodes therefore seem to contain more
overlapping speech on both sides, rather than male hosts talking over female
guests while the guests do not respond in kind.

\paragraph{Hosts vs.\ guests:}
In every dyad, guests make about three times as many successful
interruptions per 10 minutes of host speech as hosts make per 10 minutes of
guest speech (overall median 12.5 vs.\ 4.1; Table~\ref{tab:int-host-guest}).
Guests also make slightly more unsuccessful interruptions overall (5.6 vs.\
4.0). In raw counts the two roles are much closer (2{,}317 successful
interruptions by guests vs.\ 1{,}866 by hosts); the gap in rates arises
because hosts hold the floor for much less time, so each minute of host
speech is interrupted more often. Some of these ``interruptions'' may also be
guests starting to answer just before the host has finished a question.
Backchannel rates are similar for hosts and guests, except in
male$\rightarrow$male episodes, where hosts give more backchannels than
guests (17.1 vs.\ 14.2).

\subsubsection*{Conclusion}
Male hosts interrupt female guests more often than they interrupt male
guests, but this is part of a broader pattern: male$\rightarrow$female
episodes contain more interruptions and backchannels from both speakers, so
we do not read it as male hosts dominating female guests. Role matters more
than gender for who takes the floor: in every dyad, guests interrupt hosts
far more often per minute of the other speaker's speech than hosts interrupt
guests. These results are preliminary. They cover only 87 episodes from four
hosts, so dyad differences cannot be separated from differences between
individual hosts, and the male$\rightarrow$female group has only nine
episodes. The overlap types have also not yet been checked by hand. Next, we
will label the 200 sampled events to measure the accuracy of the overlap
types, and extend the analysis to all 418 episodes.

\section*{Next Steps}
Two analyses from our plan remain: interruptions and overlapping speech, and
sentiment dynamics. We will also finish the question-type analysis. All
three use the same 418 episodes, the same host$\rightarrow$guest dyads and the
same statistical approach as the completed analyses, so the results will be
directly comparable.

\subsection*{1. Completing the question type analysis}
So far, the results are preliminary, and we need more steps to align them with the topic control and dialogue act analyses.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Expand the analysis to all 418 episodes. 
  \item Re-test the hypothesis by grouping questions by episode and comparing each host's questions to male and female guests, so that differences between hosts do not drive the result.
  \item Evaluate the accuracy of the classifier by hand-labeling a sample of questions and measuring how often the classifier agrees. 

\end{itemize}

\subsection*{2. Interruptions and overlapping speech}
The results so far cover only 87 episodes from four hosts, so they are
preliminary. The remaining steps are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Expand the analysis to all 418 episodes:} Run the
    overlap-preserving diarization on episodes 1--330 (episodes 1--50 are
    queued on the GPU cluster; audio for the rest will be downloaded again
    with our scraper), then repeat the overlap detection, statistical tests
    and plots on the full corpus. This adds the other five recurring hosts
    and many more male$\rightarrow$female episodes (67 instead of 9).
  \item \textbf{Check the accuracy of the overlap types:} Listen to the 200
    sampled overlap events (40 per type) without seeing the automatic label,
    and label each one by ear. Use half of the sample to adjust the
    detection thresholds if needed, and the other half to report how often
    each automatic type is correct. Both of us will label a shared subset so
    that agreement between annotators (Cohen's $\kappa$) can be reported.
  \item \textbf{Separate host effects from gender effects:} With more hosts,
    compare each host's interruption rate with male and female guests, or
    fit a mixed-effects model with the host as a random effect, so that
    differences between individual hosts do not drive the dyad results.
  \item \textbf{Check early answers:} Test whether guests' successful
    interruptions are partly guests starting to answer before the host has
    finished a question, by checking whether the interrupted host turn ends
    in a question (using the dialogue-act labels).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{3. Sentiment and emotional dynamics}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Goal:} Test if emotional tone varies by gender dyad and how hosts and guests react to each other's emotions. (eg: Are guests more likely to mirror a male host's tone more than a female host's tone?) 
  \item Each turn will be evaluated for valence (how positive or negative it is) and arousal (how calm or intense it is) through a multilingual sentiment model. We will do this after excluding backchannels and very short turns, similar to topic control analysis. 
  \item Three behaviors will be measured: mirroring (how closely a speaker's tone aligns with the previous turn), escalation (increase in intensity across turns) and recovery (whether the host or the guest brings the tone back up
  after a negative turn). 
  \item Statistical tests to compare dyads and check host vs. guest differences within episodes. This will check sentiment measures by dyad, accuracy of the scores, and plots. 

  
\end{itemize}
