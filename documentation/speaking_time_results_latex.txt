\section*{Abstract}
\vspace{-0.8em}

    Podcasts are a rapidly growing form of digital media, especially in India, where they provide rich, multi-speaker conversational content. In this work, we provide an automated pipeline that combines gender detection, host-guest role identification, transcription, and speaker diarization to examine conversational flow in Indian podcasts. Using this framework, we analyze speaking time distribution and interaction patterns across episodes. Our results highlight gender-based disparities in interaction patterns and demonstrate that conversations are primarily guest-driven, with guests speaking much more than hosts. This work provides a structured approach for understanding real-world conversational dynamics in podcasts.

\vspace{-0.5em}


\section*{Achievements from DSCI-602 }
\vspace{-0.8em}

In the last semester, we worked on building a dataset of Indian podcast transcripts with accurate labels for the gender and role of each speaker. We first created a pipeline to scrape podcast audio files from YouTube, after which we used deep learning tools such as WhisperX and Pyannote for speaker diarization. This led to the generation of transcripts with basic speaker segmentation and corresponding timestamps. We then used a script to identify and label the gender of each speaker based on the frequency characteristics of their voice.

The next step involved identifying the host and guest in each transcript, which we implemented using the Grok API. The resulting corpus enabled us to begin identifying conversational structures, such as turn-taking and host-versus-guest dominance, and to quantify conversational dynamics, although with some limitations in accuracy. Overall, the work completed during the semester resulted in a basic but functional prototype pipeline for podcast analysis. Using this pipeline, we built a corpus of approximately $\sim 500$  transcripts.

\section*{Dataset}
\vspace{-0.8em}
The dataset consists of \textbf{418} English- and Hindi-language Indian
podcast episodes collected from YouTube, about \textbf{534 hours} of audio in
total (median episode length 70 minutes, interquartile range 54--93 minutes).
Every episode is a one-on-one conversation between a host and a guest.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Transcripts:} each episode was transcribed and diarized with
    WhisperX, giving timestamped segments attributed to speakers (about 5.1
    million words in total; median 11{,}300 words per episode).
  \item \textbf{Host and guest roles:} the host was identified by matching
    speakers' voices to a library of 9 recurring hosts (4 female, 5 male),
    which covers 389 episodes; in the remaining 29 episodes the host was
    identified with a language-model fallback.
  \item \textbf{Speaker gender:} taken from the voice library for known hosts,
    and otherwise predicted from the speaker's voice; two guests whose gender
    could not be determined were labeled manually.
  \item \textbf{Exclusions:} one episode that duplicated another was removed.
\end{itemize}
Episodes are grouped by the gender of the host and of the guest
(Table~\ref{tab:dataset-dyads}); male-hosted conversations with male guests
make up more than half of the corpus.

\section*{Host and guest speaking time by gender dyad}
\vspace{-0.8em}
For each of the 418 episodes, a speaker's speaking time is the sum of the
durations (end minus start) of their diarized WhisperX segments. The host's share of speaking time in an episode is
\[
  \text{Host share} = \frac{T_{\text{host}}}{T_{\text{host}} + T_{\text{guest}}},
\]
where $T_{\text{host}}$ and $T_{\text{guest}}$ are the host's and the guest's
speaking times in that episode.
Episodes are grouped by the host$\rightarrow$guest gender dyad. Because the
shares are skewed, we summarize each dyad by its median and interquartile range
(IQR).

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Guests speak for most of every conversation: the median host takes
    19--26\% of the host-plus-guest speaking time (Table~\ref{tab:speaking-time},
    Figures~\ref{fig:speaking-time-minutes}
    and~\ref{fig:speaking-time-share-distribution}).
  \item Male hosts speak most when the guest is a woman: their median share is
    26.3\% with female guests against 21.3\% with male guests.
  \item For female hosts the share is similar with female and male guests
    (19.8\% vs.\ 19.0\%).
  \item Episodes with male hosts are also longer: about 64--69 minutes of host
    and guest speech against 43--46 minutes with female hosts.
  \item Whether these differences between the dyads are larger than chance is
    tested in the next section.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Statistical comparison of host speaking time across dyads}
\vspace{-0.8em}
To test whether the differences in host share between the dyads (previous
section) are larger than chance, we estimate a 95\% bootstrap confidence
interval of each dyad's median host share (10{,}000 resamples of episodes).
We compare dyads with a Kruskal--Wallis test and
pairwise two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ tests, Holm-corrected across the six
pairs, with the rank-biserial correlation $r$ as the effect size.

\paragraph{Why these tests.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Why test at all.} Some difference between the four dyads'
    medians would appear by chance alone, so the tests check whether the
    observed differences are larger than the normal variation between episodes.
  \item \textbf{Kruskal--Wallis test.} A single overall test of whether any
    dyad differs from the others; pairwise comparisons are only interpreted
    when it is significant. It is the rank-based counterpart of a one-way
    ANOVA: because it compares ranks rather than raw values, it does not assume
    normally distributed data, it is not pulled by the few episodes with a very
    high host share, and it allows the unequal group sizes (226 episodes in one
    dyad, 61--67 in the others).
  \item \textbf{Pairwise Mann--Whitney $U$ tests.} The Kruskal--Wallis test
    shows that some dyads differ but not which ones, so each pair of dyads is
    then compared with a Mann--Whitney $U$ test, the two-group version of the
    same rank-based approach; the tests are two-sided because no direction was
    assumed in advance.
  \item \textbf{Holm correction.} Running six tests at the 5\% level would give
    roughly a 26\% chance of at least one false positive, so the six $p$-values
    are adjusted with the Holm method, which keeps that chance at 5\% while
    being less strict than a Bonferroni correction.
  \item \textbf{Rank-biserial correlation $r$.} A $p$-value indicates whether a
    difference is likely to be real, not how large it is, so each comparison
    also reports the rank-biserial correlation $r$: it ranges from $-1$ to
    $+1$, is $0$ when the two dyads do not differ, and measures how
    consistently episodes of one dyad have a higher host share than episodes of
    the other.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Step 1 -- Kruskal--Wallis test (do the dyads differ at all?):
    significant.} The host share differs across the four dyads
    ($H(3) = 41.4$, $p < .001$; Table~\ref{tab:speaking-time-ci},
    Figure~\ref{fig:speaking-time-share}), so the pairwise comparisons are
    interpreted.
  \item \textbf{Step 2 -- pairwise Mann--Whitney $U$ tests (which dyads
    differ?):} 4 of the 6 comparisons are significant after Holm correction
    (Table~\ref{tab:speaking-time-pairs}).

  \item \textbf{Significant differences:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Male$\rightarrow$male vs.\ male$\rightarrow$female: male hosts
        take a larger share with female guests than with male guests (21.3\%
        vs.\ 26.3\%; $r = -0.32$, Holm $p < .001$); the two confidence
        intervals do not overlap.
      \item Male$\rightarrow$female vs.\ female$\rightarrow$male: 26.3\% vs.\
        19.0\% ($r = 0.61$, Holm $p < .001$), the largest difference.
      \item Male$\rightarrow$female vs.\ female$\rightarrow$female: 26.3\% vs.\
        19.8\% ($r = 0.49$, Holm $p < .001$).
      \item Male$\rightarrow$male vs.\ female$\rightarrow$male: 21.3\% vs.\
        19.0\% ($r = 0.28$, Holm $p = .002$).
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Not significant:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Female$\rightarrow$male vs.\ female$\rightarrow$female: female
        hosts take about the same share with male and female guests (19.0\%
        vs.\ 19.8\%; Holm $p = .30$).
      \item Male$\rightarrow$male vs.\ female$\rightarrow$female: 21.3\% vs.\
        19.8\% (uncorrected $p = .037$, but Holm $p = .073$ after correction).
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{In short:} the male$\rightarrow$female dyad stands out, with a
    significantly higher host share than every other dyad. Guest gender makes
    a significant difference for male hosts but not for female hosts.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Conversational style by gender dyad}
\vspace{-0.8em}
This section describes how hosts and guests speak in each dyad: how often they
hedge, emphasize, present statements as fact, ask direct questions and
acknowledge the other speaker. For the host and the guest of each of the 418
episodes, each feature is counted and expressed as a rate per 1{,}000 words.

\paragraph{Features.}
\textbf{\emph{Hedges}} (e.g.\ \emph{I think}, \emph{maybe}, \emph{probably}) and
\emph{boosters} (e.g.\ \emph{really}, \emph{of course}, \emph{definitely}) are
taken from Hyland's metadiscourse lexicon~\cite{hyland2005metadiscourse}.
\textbf{\emph{Factuality}} (e.g.\ \emph{actually}, \emph{in fact}), \emph{deference}
(e.g.\ \emph{interesting}, \emph{nice}, \emph{great question}) and
\emph{direct questions} (wh-questions) are politeness
strategies~\cite{danescu2013politeness} as implemented in
ConvoKit~\cite{chang2020convokit}.
\paragraph{Accuracy.}
In a precision audit of 50--100 randomly sampled occurrences per feature,
71--86\% of the counted words were correct uses of the feature (hedges 86\%,
boosters 71\%, factuality 76\%, deference 82\%, host direct questions
97\%).
Guests' direct questions were mostly rhetorical in the audit, so direct
questions are analyzed for hosts only.

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Hedging.} Hosts hedge at a similar rate in all dyads (4.6--5.5
    per 1{,}000 words). Guests hedge more in episodes with a female host
    (7.1--8.2) than with a male host (4.4--4.9).
  \item \textbf{Boosters.} Boosters are more frequent in episodes with a female
    host, for hosts (5.3--6.0 vs.\ 3.1--3.5 per 1{,}000 words) and for guests.
    Female guests use more boosters than male guests with hosts of either
    gender, making FF episodes the most emphatic (guest median 6.1).
  \item \textbf{Factuality.} Guests in FF episodes use factuality markers most
    (1.75 per 1{,}000 words, against 0.74 in MM episodes); hosts use them more
    in the female-host dyads (1.28--1.55) than in MM episodes (0.96).
  \item \textbf{Direct questions.} Hosts ask more direct questions of guests of
    the other gender: male hosts ask female guests more than male guests (5.2
    vs.\ 3.3 per 1{,}000 words), and female hosts ask male guests more than
    female guests (6.4 vs.\ 3.7).
  \item \textbf{Deference.} Acknowledgements such as \emph{interesting} or
    \emph{nice} are far more common in episodes with a male host: hosts use
    them in 46--60\% of these episodes against 5--10\% with a female host, and
    guests in 44--46\% against 17--21\%.
  \item Whether these differences between the dyads are larger than chance is
    tested in the next section.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Step 1 -- Kruskal--Wallis tests (do the dyads differ at all?):
    8 of the 9 measures are significant} (Table~\ref{tab:style-kw}). Only host
    hedging does not differ across dyads ($H(3) = 3.3$, $p = .35$), so its
    pairwise comparisons are not interpreted.
  \item \textbf{Step 2 -- pairwise Mann--Whitney $U$ tests (which dyads
    differ?): 30 of the 54 comparisons are significant} after Holm correction
    (Table~\ref{tab:style-pairs}; the medians are shown in
    Figures~\ref{fig:style-rates-iqr}
    and~\ref{fig:style-questions-deference-iqr}).

    either host gender.

      \item Hosts: significant for all four female-host vs.\ male-host
        comparisons; not significant for MM--MF and FM--FF.

        ($r = -0.23$) and FM--FF ($r = -0.23$): female guests use more
        boosters than male guests with hosts of either gender.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Factuality.}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Hosts: significant only for MM--FM and MM--FF (both female-host
        dyads above MM).
      \item Guests: significant for MM--FM, MM--FF and MF--FF; not significant
        for MM--MF, MF--FM and FM--FF.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Direct questions (hosts).} Significant: MM--MF ($r = -0.27$),
    FM--FF ($r = +0.33$) and MM--FM. Not significant: MM--FF, MF--FM and
    MF--FF. Both within-host-gender comparisons are significant: hosts ask
    more direct questions of guests of the other gender.
  \item \textbf{Deference (hosts and guests).} Significant for all four
    female-host vs.\ male-host comparisons; not significant for MM--MF and
    FM--FF.
  \item \textbf{In short:} most significant differences are between episodes
    with a female host and episodes with a male host. Guest gender makes a
    significant difference within the same host gender only for guests'
    boosters and hosts' direct questions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Dialogue acts by gender dyad}
\vspace{-0.8em}
Every transcript segment of the host and the guest was labeled with one of 11
dialogue acts by a pretrained classifier (\texttt{diwank/silicone-deberta-pair},
a DeBERTa model fine-tuned on the SILICONE dialogue-act benchmark), which
reads each segment together with the preceding segment as context. Unlike the
style features, the unit is the individual transcript segment: segments are not
merged into turns, and the first and last minute of each episode are included.
For each episode we compute, separately for the host and the guest, the share
of their segments that carry each act. The comparisons use the six acts that
make up at least 1\% of all segments (Table~\ref{tab:da-dist}); the other five
are too rare to compare per episode. The \emph{ask} label covers every
question, including rhetorical and mid-turn ones, so it is broader than the
direct questions of the previous section. The dyads are compared as before:
medians with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals, a Kruskal--Wallis test per
act and speaker (Holm-corrected across the twelve tests), and pairwise
Mann--Whitney $U$ tests Holm-corrected across the six pairs, with the
rank-biserial correlation $r$ as the effect size. The classifier's accuracy on
these podcasts, which mix English and Hindi, has not been audited.

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Hosts and guests play the expected interview roles: one in five host
    segments is a question against one in thirteen guest segments, and guests'
    segments are mostly answers and statements (Table~\ref{tab:da-dist}).
  \item Ten of the twelve act-by-speaker measures differ across dyads
    (Table~\ref{tab:da}), and 33 of the 72 pairwise comparisons remain
    significant after correction (Table~\ref{tab:da-pairs}).
  \item \textbf{Guests.} The clearest difference is between episodes with
    female and male hosts.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item With a female host, guests make more statements (\emph{say}
        52.8--57.2\% of segments, against 38.0--40.8\% with a male host) and
        give fewer answers (29.6--36.3\% against 43.8--45.6\%), and they ask
        fewer questions and express fewer intentions.
      \item Guest gender matters within the female-host dyads, where female
        guests make even more statements than male guests (57.2\% vs.\ 52.8\%,
        $r = -0.32$) and give fewer answers (29.6\% vs.\ 36.3\%, $r = +0.40$).
      \item With male hosts, female guests reply ``yes'' more often than male
        guests (2.1\% vs.\ 1.5\%, $r = -0.39$); their other acts do not differ.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hosts.}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item In the female$\rightarrow$male dyad hosts ask the most questions
        (23.7\% of segments, against 20.5\% in both male-host dyads).
      \item Hosts answer less in episodes with a female host and express fewer
        intentions (three of the four comparisons with male-host dyads are
        significant).
      \item Hosts make more statements in the male$\rightarrow$female and
        female$\rightarrow$female dyads than in male$\rightarrow$male episodes.
      \item How often hosts acknowledge the guest or reply ``yes'' does not
        differ between dyads.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Topic Control and Follow-up in Mixed-Gender Dyads}
\vspace{-0.8em}

Topic control is an important aspect of conversational dominance because speakers whose topics are followed may have greater influence over the direction of the discussion. A long-standing idea in language and gender research is that men's topics are more likely to be taken up, whereas women's topics are more likely to be ignored or dropped.

Recent studies suggest that gender still shapes topic initiation \cite{TopicInitiationbyKoreanandChineseSpeakersonRealityDatingShows}, but large-scale analyses indicate that gendered speech patterns vary with context and are not consistently male-dominated \cite{yoo2022conversational}. We examine whether this pattern also appears in English-language Indian podcasts featuring one host and one guest. Podcasts are well suited to this question because they include long and natural conversations between speakers. In addition, hosts often guide the discussion through questions and guests respond to them. This helps us examine whether follow-up depends on the speaker's gender or on their conversational role.

Our analysis focuses on mixed-gender host--guest conversations and compares how often male-initiated topics receive follow-up with how often female-initiated topics receive follow-up. Since podcast transcripts do not contain direct labels for topic changes, topic initiation and follow-up are identified using lexical similarity between conversational turns, with semantic similarity used as a robustness check.



\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ:} In mixed-gender host--guest conversations, are topics introduced by male speakers followed up more often than topics introduced by female speakers?
\vspace{-0.8em}
\end{quote}

Based on prior research, we develop the following hypotheses:
\vspace{-0.8em}
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H$_1$:} Male-initiated topics receive more follow-up than female-initiated topics.

\textbf{H$_0$:} Follow-up does not differ by the gender of the topic initiator.
\end{quote}

The hypothesis test uses the \textbf{131 mixed-gender episodes} (MF + FM;
262 speakers), which contain \textbf{17,046 topic initiations} with a
response: \textbf{8,742 by male speakers} and \textbf{8,304 by female
speakers}.



\subsection*{Identifying initiations and follow-ups}
Consecutive lines from the same speaker are merged into one turn, and
backchannels such as ``yeah'' and ``mhm'' are removed. Each turn is
represented as a TF-IDF vector, and similarity between turns is measured with cosine similarity. A turn is a \emph{topic initiation} if its similarity to the previous turn is at or below the episode median. It is followed up if the other speaker's next turn is similar to it, above the episode median. Using each episode's own median keeps the measure comparable across episodes.

\subsubsection*{Statistical Analysis}
Because turns from the same episode are not independent, our primary test is a \textbf{GEE logistic regression} that predicts follow-up from the initiator's gender, controls for role (host or guest) and turn length, and groups errors by episode because turns from the same episode are not independent. We validate the result with a mixed-effects model and with paired tests comparing the male and female speakers in the same episode. Additionally, we verify the result at fixed similarity thresholds using sentence embeddings, which capture meaning rather than word overlap.

\subsubsection*{Results}

\paragraph{Raw comparison.}
Pooled across all initiations, male-initiated topics appear to be followed
up more often (42.2\% vs.\ 37.7\%; Table~\ref{tab:tc-descriptive}), and
this gap appears at every threshold (Table~\ref{tab:tc-thresholds}).
Within the same episode, however, there is no gap (41.5\% vs.\ 42.6\%;
Fig.~\ref{fig:tc-episode}).
